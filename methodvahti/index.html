Understand qualitative sampling Plain-language answers to the questions behind “how many participants do I need?” — with the terms defined.

What it is

MethodVahti helps you decide and defend a qualitative sample size. You describe your study; it synthesises three published sample-size models, estimates how varied your sample is, and drafts the methods argument — with every claim marked by how well evidence backs it.

Why it matters

Qualitative sample size has no single formula, so reviewers push on it. “Saturation” is easy to assert and hard to evidence. MethodVahti replaces a bare number with a reasoned range and a written rationale, so your N is a decision you can stand behind — not a guess.

How to use it

Pick the situation you’re in — planning an N, justifying one already set, answering a reviewer, reading pilot data, or writing methods text. It asks only for what that route needs, then returns submission-ready wording mapped to COREQ/SRQR and an audit trail of who decided what.

Who it’s for

Master’s and PhD students and their supervisors; nurses and clinician-researchers running interview or focus-group studies; anyone writing an ethics application, a protocol, a reviewer response, or a qualitative manuscript who needs the sampling section to hold up.

A worked example

A master’s student plans interviews with ward nurses about moral distress. A reviewer will ask why the N is what it is. Instead of writing “we interviewed 12 nurses until saturation”, MethodVahti works from the study: a fairly specific sample (one ward, one role), a narrow aim, and an interpretative analysis. Under information power, that combination needs relatively few participants — so a range of roughly 10–16 is defensible, and the report says why, cites the models, and notes where the case is thin.

The output isn’t a verdict that the study is sound. It’s the argument the student defends — and the record of every assumption behind it, which is exactly what COREQ and SRQR reviewers look for.

Frequently asked

How many participants do I need for a qualitative study?

There is no fixed number and no formula that settles it. Common ranges exist by design — often around 6–12 for a focused interpretative study, more for broader or more heterogeneous aims — but the honest answer depends on your research question, how specific your sample is, and how deep your analysis goes. MethodVahti turns those factors into a defensible range with the reasoning attached, rather than a single number you’d have to defend on faith.

What is data saturation — and how do I know I’ve reached it?

Saturation is the point at which new interviews stop adding anything new. It’s useful to separate code saturation (you’ve seen the range of issues) from meaning saturation (you fully understand each one) — meaning saturation usually needs more data. You can’t truly know you’ve reached it in advance, which is why planning on saturation alone is weak. MethodVahti helps you set an expectation up front and evidence the judgment afterwards from your coded records.

What is “information power”?

Information power (Malterud, Siwe & Guassora, 2016) is a way to reason about sample size without waiting for saturation. The idea: the more relevant information your sample holds, the fewer participants you need. It rises with a narrow aim, a specific sample, strong existing theory, high-quality interview dialogue, and a case-focused analysis — and falls when any of those is broad or thin. MethodVahti uses it as one of the three models behind its range.

A reviewer said my sample is too small. What can I do?

Don’t just add participants reflexively. Build the argument: state your aim’s breadth, your sample’s specificity, your analytic depth, and what saturation or information power implies for your N — and concede honestly any point that’s genuinely weak. A structured, evidence-graded rebuttal is more persuasive than a bigger number. That’s the “respond to reviewer criticism” route in MethodVahti.

Should I justify my sample size before or after data collection?

Both, and they’re different jobs. Before (in a protocol or ethics application) you argue for a planned range and a stopping rule. After (in the manuscript) you evidence what actually happened — whether you reached your stopping criterion and what the data supported. MethodVahti has separate routes for planning an N and for justifying one already set.

What’s the difference between COREQ and SRQR?

COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research, Tong et al., 2007) is a 32-item checklist specifically for interview and focus-group studies. SRQR (Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research, O’Brien et al., 2014) is a broader 21-item standard covering more qualitative designs. Many journals ask for one or the other; MethodVahti maps its output to both.

Isn’t qualitative research just “biased”? What is reflexivity?

Qualitative research is interpretative by design — the researcher is part of how meaning is made, and that’s a method, not a flaw. Reflexivity is the practice of examining how your position, assumptions, and role shape the study and reporting it openly. Far from being an admission of bias, it’s a rigor marker reviewers look for. MethodVahti records these decisions so they’re visible, not hidden.

Terms, defined

Purposive (purposeful) sampling
Choosing participants deliberately because they can speak to your question — not at random. The standard approach in qualitative work; it’s why a qualitative sample isn’t meant to be statistically representative.
Maximum variation sampling
A purposive strategy that deliberately spans a wide range of cases, so common patterns that hold across difference stand out. It typically calls for a larger N.
Data saturation
The point where additional data no longer reveals new codes or themes. Often split into code saturation (range seen) and meaning saturation (each theme fully understood).
Information power
A model (Malterud et al., 2016) for judging sample size from how much relevant information the sample holds — the more it holds, the fewer participants you need.
COREQ
A 32-item reporting checklist for interview and focus-group studies (Tong et al., 2007).
SRQR
A 21-item reporting standard for qualitative research across designs (O’Brien et al., 2014).
Reflexivity
Examining and reporting how the researcher’s position and assumptions shape the study. A rigor practice, not a confession of bias.
Member checking
Returning findings or interpretations to participants to check they ring true. One way to strengthen credibility.
Triangulation
Using more than one source, method, analyst, or theory to examine a phenomenon, so conclusions don’t rest on a single vantage point.
Thematic analysis
A widely used method for identifying, analysing, and reporting patterns (themes) across qualitative data.
Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA)
A method focused on how specific people make sense of a significant experience. It favours small, fairly homogeneous samples analysed in depth.
