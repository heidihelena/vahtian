Analysing your data

You coded everything and still have no theory

The step after themes: challenging, seeing, articulating.

The codebook is finished and the audit trail is tidy. The findings section reads like an inventory, or a badly written mechanical translation. You got five themes, each with three tidy quotes, and a discussion you keep postponing because there is nothing to discuss. You did everything the software asked of you; the software never asked for a theory.

By Heidi Andersén, MD, PhD · Updated 31 July 2026 · 8 min read

The problemThe least documented step in qualitative method

That gap has a name in the methods literature, and it is not a personal failing. Köhler, Lê and Smith call the missing step the domain of “advanced analytic techniques”, and open their 2026 paper by observing that resources for it “are largely absent from textbooks, research methods syllabi, and methods sections in empirical papers” (p. 2). What the tools and templates cover well is the lower half of the work: import, transcribe, code, retrieve, count. The step that makes a study worth reading, the conceptual leap “from the mass of words … to an abstract and explicit set of concepts, relations and explanations” (Klag & Langley 2013, p. 150, quoted on p. 2), is the least documented part of qualitative method. Their explanation is uncomfortable: the field’s push for standardized templates has made researchers hide their actual discovery process, because stumbling and leaping look less scientific than following a procedure (pp. 3, 25–26).

The distinctionImprovement is not transformation

Quantitative work asks how well an existing theory survives measurement. Qualitative work asks what theory becomes possible once the existing one no longer explains what you observed. If your analysis only ever confirms and refines the framework you brought in, the coding may be immaculate and the study still ends where it began. The codebook is raw material for a claim you have not made yet.

Köhler and colleagues build their framework on abduction, Peirce’s third mode of inference: deduction proves what must be, induction shows what is, “abduction merely suggests that something may be” (Peirce, quoted on p. 5). The process runs on three moves. What follows is their framework with the researcher’s actual behaviour attached.

ChallengingTake the surprise seriously

Theorizing starts when something in the material refuses to fit. An unexpected observation, a divergent case, a questioning comment during a member check: the anomaly “draws into doubt what the researcher thought they knew” and produces what the paper calls generative doubt, a motivated state of not knowing (p. 6). In their first worked example, a research team noticed managers repeatedly using the term “end-to-end management” for coordination, a usage that felt unusual to them as management scholars. Instead of coding past it, they made the odd term itself the object: retrieved every mention, preserved context and temporal order, and let the anomaly restructure the analysis (p. 12).

The practical move: when the data surprises you, write the surprise down before you resolve it, together with the explanation it just defeated. A surprise you code over is gone by the next session.

SeeingRun an analytic experiment

Seeing is “new ways of making sense of some aspect of existing social worlds” (Klag & Langley 2013, quoted on p. 7), and it is produced by moves. The paper’s examples are concrete. Arranging the end-to-end data month by month exposed that the concept was evolving for the participants themselves (p. 13). In the second example, coding meeting transcripts thematically produced only superficial differences between German and American teams; building activity logs, minutes spent per activity in order, made the patterns “literally jump out”: different rhythms, content, and participation, replicated across independent tasks (pp. 17–18). In the third, temporal bracketing and visual maps of six firms’ international expansion turned a mass of archival data into comparable phases, and the “squiggles” where expansion stalled became the finding (pp. 20–22).

None of these moves is exotic. Each is an experiment: a conjecture about what might be going on, an arrangement of the material that would show it, and a result that confirms, complicates, or kills the conjecture. The authors’ word for choosing moves is methodological bricolage, “the combining of analytic moves for the purpose of solving a problem or problems tailored to one’s own research project” (Pratt et al. 2022, quoted on p. 7). There is no fixed sequence to comply with; there is a fit between question, setting, data, and move. My epistemic notes have a flow, and that flow is the analytic experiment.

ArticulatingWrite the claim so it can be examined

The third process is getting the insight out of your head in a form others can inspect: writing, visualizing, presenting, iterating (p. 10). In the expansion study this took years, several rejected framings, and reviewer pressure before “sensegiving followed by sensemaking” emerged as the explanation that held (pp. 22–23). Articulation is analysis: each attempt to state the claim exposes where it exceeds the material.

In practiceKeep the path in plain files

Everything above can live in the same folder as your transcripts, as one note type. We call it a conceptual leap note: one file per surprise, from first observation to theoretical consequence.

# Conceptual leap — [title]

## Observation        — what you saw; quote it, with source
## Surprise           — why it stopped you; what you expected instead
## Existing explanation — the theory that should have covered this, and where it falls short
## Generative doubt   — the open question, written as a question
## Conjecture         — what might be going on (dated, signed speculation)
## Analytic move      — what you did to test it, and on which data
## Result             — confirmed, complicated, or killed; say which
## Alternative explanations — what you considered and did not adopt, and why
## Theoretical consequence  — what you can now claim, and where the claim stops

A note template does not produce theory; researchers theorized for a century without one, and a study can be genuinely theoretical with this file empty. The note earns its place on survivability. Köhler and colleagues found that the discovery process goes unreported partly because, by writing time, no record of it exists (pp. 25–26). When a reviewer asks how you got from the codes to the claim, this note is an answer with a date on it. It also protects you in both failure directions: an empty “Analytic move” section shows interpretation outrunning the material, and a vault full of codes with no leap notes shows procedure standing in for discovery.

Tension to know about: Köhler and colleagues argue that generative AI cannot deliver challenging, seeing, or articulating at all, and wrote their paper without it (pp. 28–29, 31). Other methodologists take a softer line, AI as an interlocutor that argues with you while you decide (our reading of that debate). Wherever you land, the leap note is upstream of the dispute: its content only counts if you wrote it. You as a human reflect the world from your perspective; my exercise for you is to change the perspective into how your research objectives see the world.

Set it upWith your agent, or with epistemic notes

If you keep your analysis in plain files, paste this to your AI agent:

I keep my qualitative analysis in plain markdown files. Create a file called
"Conceptual leap note template.md" in my templates folder with these sections:
Observation, Surprise, Existing explanation, Generative doubt, Conjecture,
Analytic move, Result, Alternative explanations, Theoretical consequence.
Add a one-line comment under each saying what belongs there.

Do not fill in any section for me, and never suggest conjectures, explanations,
or theory: a leap note only counts if I wrote it. After I have written one, your
job is limited to checking structure: flag any empty section, check that
"Analytic move" names data I can point to, and check that "Alternative
explanations" contains at least one explanation I did not adopt. Report
what you find; I decide what it means.

Most qualitative software assumes the value lies in organising data. My vault assumes the value lies in tracking epistemic movement. The real contribution comes from the movement between observation, doubt, imagination, and theory, what they call challenging, seeing, and articulating.
