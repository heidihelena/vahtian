Registered Reports: reviewed before you run the study

The most expensive sentence in research arrives at peer review: this design could not have answered this question. By then the data is collected, the funding is spent, and the sentence cannot be acted on. Registered Reports deliver that sentence at the point where it still costs a week.

By Heidi Andersén, MD, PhD. Updated 1 August 2026.9 minute read

The Registered Report is one of the biggest changes in scientific publishing of the past decade, and it works in two stages.

Stage 1, before data collection

You submit the study before it exists:

  • the research question and the rationale for it,
  • the hypotheses,
  • the methods,
  • the sample size justification,
  • the statistical analysis plan.

Peer reviewers evaluate whether the question is important and whether the methods are rigorous. There are no results to read, so there is nothing else to judge on.

If the study passes review, the journal gives an in-principle acceptance. At that point the journal has committed to publishing the paper, provided that:

  • you follow the approved protocol,
  • you collect the data as planned,
  • you report the results honestly.

It is a commitment, not a certainty. The Center for Open Science describes in-principle acceptance as the journal virtually guaranteeing publication if the study is conducted in accordance with the approved protocol. The conditions above are the whole of it: depart from the protocol without disclosing and explaining the departure and the commitment is not what protects you. What the journal has given up is the right to reject you for the direction of your results.

Stage 2, after the study

Reviewers check whether you followed the protocol and whether the conclusions match the evidence. They do not judge the paper on whether the results are positive, statistically significant, or exciting.

What the format reduces

Moving the review earlier takes the incentive out of four well-known problems at once:

  • Publication bias. The decision to publish is made before the results exist, so the results cannot influence it. Negative and null findings become as publishable as positive ones.
  • P-hacking. The analysis plan is fixed before anyone can see which analysis flatters the result. You can still run exploratory analyses. You have to label them as what they are.
  • HARKing, hypothesising after the results are known. The hypotheses are on the record before there is any data for them to be fitted to.
  • Selective reporting. The outcomes were named at Stage 1, so an outcome that goes missing at Stage 2 is visible as missing.

There is a fifth effect that gets less attention, and for most authors it is the one that pays: design criticism arrives while the design can still change. The reviewer who would have written "underpowered for this comparison" in a rejection letter writes it while you can still fix the sampling.

Nothing here is a new virtue in reviewers. It is the same review, applied at a different moment. It does not make a study true either. It makes the record of what you intended checkable against what you did, which is a different and more modest thing.

Is it the right format for your study?

It fits confirmatory work: a stated hypothesis, a design chosen to test it, and data not yet collected. It fits badly where the work is genuinely exploratory, and forcing exploratory work into a confirmatory frame is its own distortion. Some journals also offer the format for meta-analysis or for secondary analysis of existing data, where the constraint becomes committing to the analysis before you look.

Which journals offer it

Many respected journals do, including titles published by Nature Portfolio (selected Nature journals), Springer Nature, PLOS, BMC, the Royal Society, BMJ, Elsevier, Wiley, SAGE and APA.

Read that as journals rather than as publishers. The format is adopted title by title, so one journal from a publisher offering it tells you nothing about the journal next to it. The Center for Open Science keeps the registry, more than 300 journals at the time of writing, some as a standing submission option and some for a single special issue: cos.io/initiatives/registered-reports. Check your target journal there, then read its own Stage 1 instructions, because what Stage 1 must contain varies between them.

Two checkpoints, two different questions

A Registered Report and a pre-submission check are often confused because both involve a checklist. They sit at opposite ends of the study and answer different questions.

Registered ReportPre-submission check
QuestionAm I about to run the wrong study?Is the finished manuscript ready to go to reviewers?
WhenBefore data collectionAfter the study is written up
Who looksJournal reviewers, at Stage 1You, before any reviewer sees it
What it changesThe design, while changing it is still cheapAvoidable desk rejections and reviewer criticism
What it coversQuestion, hypotheses, methods, sample size justification, analysis planConsistency between claims, methods, statistics, figures, reporting-guideline items, ethics and funding statements, references, data availability
What neither doesDecide whether your findings are correct. One checks the plan, the other checks the report of what happened.

They are not alternatives. A study can go through both, and a Stage 2 submission still benefits from the second one.

Which checklist belongs to which stage

This trips people up, and it is worth being blunt about. A Stage 1 submission is a protocol, not a completed study. The completed-study checklists describe things that have not happened yet, so they are the wrong instrument:

What you are submittingChecklist
Stage 1 protocol, trialSPIRIT
Stage 1 protocol, systematic reviewPRISMA-P
Completed randomised trialCONSORT 2025
Completed observational studySTROBE
Completed systematic reviewPRISMA 2020
Qualitative interviews or focus groupsCOREQ
Anything elseSearch by study type at EQUATOR Network

Filing a Stage 1 protocol against CONSORT produces a document full of items you cannot answer yet, and it reads to an editor as though you have misunderstood the format.

How to use a checklist

Most advice stops at "complete the checklist for your study type". The part that matters is how, and it is not obvious:

  1. Fill it while you write, not after. Used at the end it is a compliance form and it changes nothing, because the paper is already written. Used while writing it is a design instrument: an item you cannot answer is usually a section you have not thought through, found at the point where thinking is still cheap.
  2. A mark is your attestation, not a finding. When you tick an item you are telling an editor that your paper reports that item adequately. That is a claim you are making about your own work, and an editor relies on it. It is not the same kind of object as a tool telling you a sentence exists.
  3. Presence is not adequacy. Finding a funding sentence in your manuscript tells you a sentence is there. Whether it says what this journal requires is a reading you have to do.
  4. Do not let anything fill it in for you. A tool, or an AI agent, can tell you what it can see in the text. It cannot attest on your behalf. Transcribing its findings into your checklist means attesting to something you have not checked, and the attestation is the part the editor is relying on.
  5. Give the location, not just the tick. Journals ask where each item is reported, by page or section. If you cannot point at a place, the item is not reported yet, whatever the tick says.

Where a tool helps, and where it does not

Before data collection, the part of Stage 1 that is hardest to defend is usually the sample size justification. A conventional power calculation answers one question: at this n, how often would an effect of this size be detected? It does not tell you whether attrition will quietly change the estimand you set out to measure, whether the estimator stays calibrated under the missingness you expect, or whether the estimates that do reach significance are exaggerated.

RecoverLite tests those by simulation. You declare the target estimand, the data strategy, the measurement and missingness models, and the planned analysis. It generates data across a crossed grid of null and target effects, each under your declared assumptions and under a pessimistic perturbation of them, applies the planned analysis to every simulated dataset, and turns the result into a PASS, RISK or FAIL verdict under a versioned threshold profile. The verdict is recomputed under a stricter and a more lenient profile, and one that flips between profiles is itself a finding, which is the honest way to report a design sitting on a line. It runs offline, as an R package from r-universe or a Python package from PyPI, and it is a prototype implementation of a working-draft protocol rather than a settled method.

A PASS there is evidence about the instrument, not about the world. It does not test the assumptions you declared, and it says nothing about people. That distinction is worked through at forskai.com, which publishes a pre-data checklist for this moment and offers a reviewed design service for people who would rather have a design stress-tested than self-test it.

After the study, the Pre-Submission Check runs the second checkpoint in the browser: the statement scan, the reporting-guideline item walk, and links out to the reference check, which resolves identifiers against Crossref, DataCite and OpenLibrary, and to the claim-wording and claim-to-source steps. It flags what is open for you to judge. It does not decide whether the manuscript is ready, because that is not a thing a machine can settle.

If you work with an AI agent

An agent can run parts of the second checkpoint and none of the first. The honest instruction is the one that makes it say so:

Use the Vahtian presubmission-check skill:
https://raw.githubusercontent.com/heidihelena/vahtian/main/skill/presubmission-check/SKILL.md

My manuscript is finished. Run the steps you can run, list every step
you cannot as "not run" with its link, and give me the list of what to
fix. Do not mark any reporting-guideline item for me: those marks are
my attestation to the editor. Do not give me a readiness verdict.

The skill is written so that an agent reports the steps it cannot run rather than approximating them, which is the failure mode worth guarding against: an agent that quietly re-implements a check and reports the guess as a tool result. More on agent use at Vahtian for agents.

You do not need our tools for any of this. The format, the checklists, and the EQUATOR guidelines are all free and published by other people.
