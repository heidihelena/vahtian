Respond to reviewers without losing a comment

A reviewer has put three requests inside one sentence and labelled the section “minor comments”. The job is not to write more. It is to show that every point has a response, a real change, and a place the editor can find it.

By Heidi Andersén, MD, PhD. Updated 16 July 2026.4 minute read

After this guide you will be able to:

  • Answer every comment, including the buried sub-points.
  • Pick a defensible stance for each one.
  • Avoid the two faults that get a revision rejected.

Minor comments.Three requests and a citation query.

Coverage is the hard part, not the letter

The letter itself is manageable. Coverage is the hard part, especially when a small request is tucked after a semicolon halfway through a paragraph. Editors expect a point-to-point response: each comment answered on its own, in order, not folded into a summary. So do not write prose first. Split the reviews into single comments, one per row. Then give each comment four things: a stance, a response, the exact change, and where that change lives. The sub-point you miss is remarkably likely to be the reviewer’s favourite.

The four stances

Every comment gets one of four stances. A reasoned mix is more credible to an editor than a wall of automatic agreement.

StanceUse it when
Acceptthe reviewer is right; you made the change.
Partlyyou agree with part of it and made a smaller or different change.
Disagreeyou have a reason, grounded in evidence, not just a preference.
Clarifythe comment rests on a misreading; you explain, and often improve the text so the misreading cannot recur.

The two failure modes

Most revisions fail on one of two faults. Check for both before you submit.

A disagreement with no reason. "We disagree" with nothing after it is what a reviewer digs into. If you disagree, state the reason and cite it. If you cannot, that is a sign you should concede.

A claim with no change. Saying you addressed a comment without a real, findable change in the manuscript. An editor cross-checks. Every "we have revised" needs a location.

The hard cases

Three come up often. Contradictory reviewers: name the tension, pick the change your study can support, and explain the choice to the editor rather than trying to satisfy both. A hostile comment: answer the substance plainly and leave the tone where you found it; the editor is reading. A limit you cannot meet — a re-analysis you have no data for: say so plainly, do what you can, and state the residual limitation. Conceding a real limit is stronger than pretending.

The discipline underneath. Disagree with evidence, concede without gutting the paper, and answer a hostile comment without escalating. Never fake a change, invent a citation, or over-concede to appease. A response letter is judged on coverage and reasons, and both can be checked before you submit.
