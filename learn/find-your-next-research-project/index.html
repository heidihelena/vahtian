Find your next research project without another brainstorm

You already have ideas. They are in notebooks, half-analysed datasets, and the AI chat that produced twelve more before lunch. The useful question is not “what could we study?” but “which gap survives checking?”

By Heidi Andersén, MD, PhD. Updated 25 July 2026.5 minute read

After this guide you will be able to:

  • Lay a field out as a small, decidable map instead of a list.
  • Audit each part as evidenced, assumed, unknown, or a real gap.
  • Pick a next project you can defend, not just the newest idea.

We need more ideas.We need a way to choose.

Your idea list is not a strategy

This is not a discipline problem. Nothing in a normal workflow forces the check between an interesting idea and a defensible project, so every idea stays open and the newest one always looks strongest. A structure for deciding closes that loop: once the ideas share one map, they stop competing and start sorting themselves.

The method: one thesis, a few axes, a grid

Structure beats brainstorming. Do it in three moves.

  1. Name one core thesis. The single claim your field, or your corner of it, turns on.
  2. Choose a few axes. The two or three dimensions along which that thesis varies: population, method, mechanism, setting, whatever your field cuts by.
  3. Fill the grid. Each combination of axis values is a cell, a possible project. Now you are looking at the space, not a pile of ideas.

Then audit every cell

This is the step that turns a map into a decision. Give each cell one of four labels:

MarkMeaning
EvidencedSomeone has done this; the evidence exists. Not a gap.
AssumedEveryone believes it; check whether anyone has shown it.
UnknownOpen, but perhaps unanswerable or not worth the years.
Real gapOpen, answerable, and worth doing. Your candidate projects.

The audit is the point. A gap you only assumed is where wasted years go: you build a project on "no one has looked at this", and a reviewer finds the three papers that did. Marking each cell forces the check before you commit.

Why the map is worth the hour. A finished map does two jobs at once. It shows you the real gaps, and it shows a supervisor or a funder that you looked at the whole field before choosing, which is exactly the case a grant has to make.
