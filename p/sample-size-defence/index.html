The reviewer wants to know why six interviews were enough

"The reviewers stated that there was no explanation for data saturation." Rejection letters keep saying some version of that sentence, because the sample-size question is the easiest one for a qualitative reviewer to ask and the hardest to answer from memory.

It is rarely a flaw in the study. The word saturation travels between research traditions without its definition attached, so authors inherit the term, reviewers inherit the question, and the manuscript holds neither the logic nor the record. Six interviews can be a defensible sample. Sixty can be an indefensible one. What decides it is whether the justification was made, in words, and written down.

What a defensible justification contains

The logic. Name the reasoning your tradition accepts: information power, a defined saturation criterion, or a purposive design rationale. One, stated plainly.

The number. Connect the logic to your N and your question, not to a rule of thumb from another field.

The record. Date the decision. A justification written before or during data collection reads as a plan; one reconstructed at review reads as an excuse — even when it is honest.

You can do all of this with a blank document. The hard part is knowing what your tradition's accepted logic is, phrasing it in words a reviewer recognises, and keeping the record as you go.
